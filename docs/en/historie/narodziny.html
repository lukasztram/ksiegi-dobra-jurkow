A Calendar of Births

More than 25,000 baptisms in the parish registers show in which months children were born and how quickly they were carried to the font.

Translated from the Polish original with the help of Claude. I give quotations from the registers in the original, with a translation.

Children were born all year round, but not evenly. The transcribed baptism registers of the parish from 1678–1909 record more than 25,000 children with a full date of birth or baptism (as of November 2026).

January and February

The most children came into the world in January and February: more than a fifth above an average month. The fewest in July and August, roughly a fifth below average.

Just count back nine months. The most children were conceived in spring, in April and May, and the fewest in the fall, in October and November.

Why

The registers do not say. The abstinence recommended during the fasting seasons probably made little difference. Paweł Pietrowcew, who compared data from many parishes of the past, showed that because weddings were held just before Lent and Advent, young couples conceived children precisely during the fasting seasons, even though the Church recommended abstinence at those times. In our parish, too, most weddings took place in February and November, as the story “A Calendar of Weddings” tells.

The spring peak and the fall low in conceptions may have been connected with the seasons and with the hard fieldwork of the autumn harvest. But that is only a guess.

In earlier times the difference was greater

In the oldest registers, from 1678–1785, twice as many children were born in February and March as in July. In the second half of the 19th century the difference between months was smaller: almost one and a half times as many children were born in January as in August.

Straight to baptism

The 19th-century registers give the day of birth and the day of baptism separately. In 1811–1880 more than nine children in ten were baptized on the day they were born or the day after. Until the middle of the century, half were baptized that very same day. People hurried because infants often died, as the story “The First Year of Life” tells.

Later, baptism was put off more and more often, by a day, two, or three. In 1881–1909 only one child in four was baptized on the day of birth, and in 1910–1923 one in nine. By then more than one child in four was baptized only after two or three days.

How certain is this

  • CertainThe overall picture: most births in winter and early spring, the fewest in summer. Until the mid-19th century, baptism almost always on the day of birth or the day after.
  • ProbableThe exact numbers and percentages, counted automatically from transcriptions of the registers of all nine villages, as of November 2026. After Porąbka, Wilczyce, Zadziele, and Łostówka were added, the proportions between months stayed the same. The oldest registers give only the date of baptism. If children were baptized as quickly then as in the 19th century, the picture does not change.
  • PossibleThe explanations of why the most children were conceived in spring and the fewest in the fall.

What we don’t know

  • Why conceptions were fewest in the fall: because of the hard fieldwork, or for other reasons.
  • How quickly children were baptized in the 17th and 18th centuries. The oldest registers give only the day of baptism.

Sources

  • All transcribed baptism registers of the Dobra and Jurków parishes from 1678–1909 (collections 4268 and 4269), and the annual copies of the Dobra and Jurków registers from 1910–1923 (collection 3402), on the Metryki website of the Polish Genealogical Society (PTG).
  • P. Pietrowcew, “Wpływ kalendarza liturgicznego na sezonowość poczęć w nowożytnej Polsce i na Śląsku” (The Influence of the Liturgical Calendar on the Seasonality of Conceptions in Early Modern Poland and Silesia), “Przeszłość Demograficzna Polski” 45 (2023).
