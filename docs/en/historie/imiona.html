Named by the Calendar

More than 25,000 baptisms make it possible to see where children’s names came from and how fashions in names changed.

Translated from the Polish original with the help of Claude. I give quotations from the registers in the original, with a translation.

Where did children’s names come from? The registers answer this question with numbers. The transcribed baptism registers of the parish contain more than twenty-eight thousand children and about three hundred different first names (as of November 2026). Many of them came straight from the calendar.

A name from the day of birth

If names had nothing to do with the date, roughly one in twenty-five children with a given name would have been born in the week before or after that name’s saint’s day. Instead:

  • Melchior · January 69 out of 10 Melchiors were born in the week before or after the feast of the Three Kings (Epiphany).
  • Łukasz · October 18almost 8 out of 10.
  • Szczepan · December 262 out of 3.
  • Walenty · February 14more than half; the same for Wawrzyniec, August 10.
  • Marcin, Mikołaj, Barbara, Błażejmore than half near their patron saint’s day.

The parents, or perhaps rather the priest, took the name of the saint whose day fell just before or just after the birth. That is why the Błażejs were born in early February and the Barbaras in early December. Names for twins were chosen the same way, in pairs of saints, as the story “Twins” tells.

The most popular names were given all year round. Jans, Józefs, Katarzynas, and Annas were born in every month. In a parish whose patron saints are the apostles Szymon and Juda Tadeusz (Simon and Jude Thaddeus), boys were often named Szymon, and one in four of them was born in the week of the parish feast, around October 28.

Fashions in names

Names had their fashions too. At the end of the 17th century, one girl in five in the parish was a Regina. By the mid-19th century there were hardly any Reginas left, and after 1900 the registers know of only two, both from Wilczyce, from 1915 and 1918. In the early 18th century Zofia was even more popular than Regina, and a hundred years later it was rare.

Before 1800 there are almost no Kunegundas. By the mid-19th century, one girl in seven bore the name. Kunegunda did not follow the calendar; Kunegundas were born all year round. Perhaps this was the influence of the cult of Blessed Kinga, that is, Kunegunda, from nearby Stary Sącz. Toward the end of the 19th century, on the other hand, the number of Helenas grew.

Among boys, Jan always came first. After 1825, one boy in five was given this name. Józef was less common for a long time, but in the second half of the 19th century nearly one boy in five bore it. Sebastian, popular in the 18th century, becomes rare after 1850.

One name, four names

Peasant children were usually given a single name. Fewer than one child in twenty-five in the registers has two names. It was different in the families of officials and manor foresters who arrived toward the end of the 19th century.

These families also brought names the parish had never known before. At house no. 202 in Dobra, where a family from Moravia lived, the children baptized in 1895–1904 included Włodzimierz, Otakar, Jarosław, and Mojmir. The eldest of them is the subject of the story “The Innkeeper’s Grandson”.

More than a hundred names appear in the registers only once or twice. Almost every other child with such a name was born out of wedlock, although among all the baptized only about one in eleven was. In those cases the name was often taken from the calendar, from the day of birth. We do not name those children here.

How certain is this

  • CertainThe quoted entries. Readings compared with the scans.
  • ProbableThe numbers and percentages, as of November 2026. They were counted automatically from transcriptions of the baptism registers of all nine villages from 1678–1923 and may differ slightly from an exact count.
  • PossibleThat the fashion for Kunegunda came from the cult of Blessed Kinga.

What we don’t know

  • Who really chose the name: the parents, the godparents, or the priest. The registers do not say.
  • What names people used in everyday life. The priest wrote them in Latin, while in the village people said Wojtek, Maciek, Kaśka (familiar forms of Wojciech, Maciej, and Katarzyna).
