Dobra and the whole Dobra estate · 1726–1732 · a new story from the registers and court records

The Heaped Bushel

For years the parish priest of Dobra collected his grain dues with a measure that held one and a half times as much grain as was due. Peasants from the whole Dobra estate took him to court and won, and the priest lost his parish.

Translated from the Polish original with the help of Claude. I give quotations from the registers in the original, with a translation.

“Meszne” was a tribute in grain that the peasants gave the parish priest once a year, in the fall. In Dobra parish a full farmer gave a “korzec” (a measure of grain) of wheat and a korzec of oats from his holding, and a “zarębnik,” who farmed a cleared patch of forest, a korzec of oats. The dispute, which dragged on for years, was not about whether to give. It was about which korzec to measure with.

A new measure

Under the parish priest Walenty Borwiński, who died in 1692, the grain was measured with a stamped Wieliczka measure that held four quarters. After his death the wójt (village head) of Dobra, Wojciech Przybytek, burned the parsonage’s measure. He had heard that tithes were being abolished in the country, and he wanted to free people from the meszne.

The new parish priest, Father Wojciech Juraszewski, had a cooper make a new measure, and then a second, larger one. It held five quarters, and with the “obsypka,” that is, heaped, as many as six. People grumbled but paid, because they did not want the parish priest for an enemy. This went on for years.

Finally the village head, Łukasz Michałek, and the village elders complained to the lady of the manor, Anna Małachowska. Her answer was short: what the parish priest had found in place was hard to abolish, but what he had raised on his own was “easy to abolish and cancel.”

A korzec from Kraków

The village community took its complaint to the bishop’s court, the consistory, in Kraków. The verdict came on July 12, 1726. The meszne was to be measured with a small Kraków korzec of four quarters, level, “strychem” (struck off flat with a strickle), with no heap, and handed over every year on St. Martin’s Day (November 11). The peasants brought the consistory’s measure back from Kraków and from then on measured with it.

The parish priest did not accept the verdict. He refused to take grain measured with it and appealed to the Apostolic Nunciature in Warsaw. He also accused the village head, Michałek, of stirring people up. The community took the case to the castle court (“sąd grodzki”) in Nowy Sącz as well.

Witnesses in the church

In May 1731 a commission of the nunciature came to Dobra. The witnesses were questioned in the church. First to testify were people connected with the parsonage: the 71-year-old cantor Jan Godulski, the 50-year-old Karol Godulski, and the priest’s clerk, the nobleman Antoni Borucki. They defended the parish priest.

In June it was the others’ turn: a 90-year-old lodger from Dobra, full farmers, and zarębnicy from Chyszówki and Wilczyce. They told how the parsonage korzec, heaped, took six quarters, while in Tymbark, Słopnice, and Skrzydlna people gave only four. The parish priest refused to hear the Easter confession of anyone who gave short measure. When the peasants drove off with the meszne to the manor, the curate ran after them with a crucifix, shouting that lightning should strike them.

The baptism register shows who stood by whom. In 1729 the parish priest himself was godfather to the daughter of his clerk, Borucki.

In the same register Karol Godulski is recorded as bell-ringer, and Jan Godulski as rector of the school and later as cantor. The curate Andrzej Migalski, with whom the village head is said to have argued “without uncovering his head,” performed baptisms in 1724–1725.

The verdict

The nunciature turned the case around. Now it was the parish priest who became the accused, because complaints about his life and past deeds had reached it. In December 1731 another commission arrived. Several hundred angry people gathered outside the church. Witnesses testified that around 1707 the parish priest had beaten and kicked a farmer named Mysza from Wilczyce, who died a few days later. They also spoke of parishioners who, through the priest’s fault, had died without the sacraments, and of enormous fees for funerals.

In January 1732 the official general in Kraków took the parish of Dobra away from Father Juraszewski. He sentenced him to a month’s retreat with the Missionary Fathers at Stradom in Kraków and ordered him to say 34 Masses for the dead. The priest moved to Sułkowice near Myślenice. In the spring of 1733 the curate left Dobra by night, taking with him several months of baptism records, as the story “The Register Begins with a Fire” tells. Whether this was connected with the turmoil is not known.

Father Juraszewski was also a good steward of the church. He brought in an organ, had the high altar gilded, and built an altar for the icon of the Transfiguration of the Lord. As Wojtusiak writes, the flood of 1712 carried the icon away from Jurków, and it was found on the stony riverbank below the church in Dobra. On the painting of Our Lady of the Scapular he had himself painted as the kneeling donor. The parishioners demanded that his successor paint over the portrait. It came out from under the paint again during conservation in 1952.

A few years later the peasants of the Dobra estate took on the manor’s steward in another dispute. The story “Forty-One Charges” tells about it.

How certain is this

  • CertainEntries from the baptism register: the village head Łukasz Michałek, the Godulskis, the baptism in the Borucki family, and the curate Migalski’s signatures. Readings compared with the scans.
  • ProbableThe course of the dispute, the witness testimony, and the verdict. We know them from the article by Edward Wojtusiak, who described the court records. We have not seen the records themselves.
  • PossibleA link between the curate’s departure by night in 1733 and the parish priest’s case.
  • To be checkedThe spelling of the parish priest’s surname: Juraszewski in Wojtusiak, “Jurasowski” in the baptism register.

What we don’t know

  • What the first name of the farmer Mysza from Wilczyce was. The court records give Wojciech in one place and Andrzej in another. His death falls in years from which the baptism register has not survived, and the death registers begin only in 1779.
  • How much grain a korzec held at the time. Measures differed from town to town and from region to region.
