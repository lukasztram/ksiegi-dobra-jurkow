The manor in Dobra had its own people, and the parish registers know them by name. From at least 1829 the estate was held by a leaseholder (“posesor”), Józef Kisielewski. He lived at house no. 1. His wife, Kunegunda, née Chełbinowska, stood godmother to village children, peasant children among them. Godparents from the manor of this kind are the subject of the story {{tytul:hrabia-przy-chrzcielnicy}}.

The manor’s official

The other important person at the manor was the mandatary (“mandatariusz”). In Austrian Galicia, each village, or several villages belonging to one lord, formed a dominium. The dominium carried out the orders of the authorities of the “cyrkuł,” the district: it collected taxes and took recruits for the army. The lord could do this himself or through a paid official, the mandatary. For the peasants this made the manor a hated instrument of state power, and the mandatary the man who carried out its orders.

In the 1830s the mandatary in Dobra was Aleksander Garbiński, also recorded as “judex politicus,” political judge. He died in Jurków in January 1841 of pneumonia, at the age of 52. Earlier disputes between the villages and the manor’s people are the subject of the story {{tytul:rzadca}}.

The winter of 1846

In February 1846 Polish conspirators were preparing an uprising against Austria. But in western Galicia something else broke out: the “rabacja,” known in English as the Galician Slaughter. From February 19 on, armed bands of peasants attacked manor houses. Within a few days they had looted and destroyed more than 500 manors, including some in the Bochnia and Sącz districts. The peasants also besieged towns, Limanowa among them. Between 1,200 and 3,000 people were killed, almost exclusively landowners and manorial and government officials.

On March 1, 1846, the parish priest of Dobra entered the mandatary in the death register.

The priest did not write who killed him or where. There is no date of burial either, because he did not bury the dead man himself. As the one who buried the mandatary, he entered another parish priest, Franciszek Głuziński. Why he was buried in Bochnia, the register does not say.

The date and the words “by reason of a crime” point to a link between this death and the rabacja. He died ten days after it broke out, and at that time manorial officials, along with landowners, were the main target of the attacks.

The widow and the daughter

The mandatary left behind in Dobra his wife Teodozja, née Wentkowska, and his daughter Leokadia. The year of famine did not spare their home. On August 12, 1847, seven-year-old Leokadia died of dysentery. The priest still called her father the local mandatary. That summer is the subject of the story {{tytul:rok-glodu}}.

In October 1847 the widow, recorded as “vidua Mandatarii Dobrensis,” widow of the mandatary of Dobra, stood godmother to the son of the manor’s farm manager Wincenty. The child was born in house no. 114, the same house where her husband had lived a year and a half earlier.

Successors

The manor soon had a new official. As early as April 1847 the death register mentions the mandatary Antoni Przorad, whose daughter Emilia, a year and a half old, had died. In 1850 the mandatary was Jan Majerski, and his wife Helena stood godmother to village children.

Meanwhile much more had changed. In 1848 the “pańszczyzna,” the labor that peasants owed the manor, was abolished in Galicia.

What we don’t know

  • Who killed the mandatary, where, and why he was buried in Bochnia.
  • Whether peasants from our villages took part in the rabacja, and whether they attacked the manor in Dobra.
  • Who held the estate in 1846. The registers still mention the leaseholder Kisielewski in 1844.
