For most of the 19th century, children in our villages were brought into the world by neighbor women. They had no schooling and no examination, but they had experience: some delivered several hundred babies.

From March 1832 the baptism registers have a separate column for the midwife. In the first weeks of the new form, in all the villages of the parish, from Gruszowiec to Chyszówki, the priest wrote the same thing there: “non examinata,” not examined. In Jurków it was Agnieszka Syrszeń, in Dobra Franciszka Pałka, in Chyszówki Katarzyna Brzeń.

Hundreds of births for one woman

The midwives’ names over the years show how much work fell to a single woman. The numbers are the baptisms at which her name stands in the register.

  • Jurków · 1832–1858Agnieszka Syrszeń: at least 238 children.
  • Jurków and Półrzeczki · 1871–1907Anna Rapacz: more than 300 children in two villages.
  • JurkówTeresa Smoleń, Marianna Jania, and Marianna Michałek: more than a hundred births each.
  • Chyszówki · 1866–1890Zofia Czopek: 183 children.
  • Dobra · 1850–1877Rozalia Przybytek: 274 children. She also delivered the children of Jewish neighbors, as the story {{tytul:sasiedzi}} tells.
  • Dobra · 1885–1901Marianna Matacz, usually recorded as Maria from 1890 on: about 570 children, if this is the same person.
  • Dobra · 1901–1923Agnieszka Chalcarz: 739 children, the most of all.

The midwife also did what the priest could not do when every minute counted. If a newborn was weak, she baptized it at home right away, “with water.” In November 1829 in Jurków, for the child of Franciszek Kulig and Zofia, née Żak, the priest wrote only this:

Trained midwives

Midwives who had passed an examination, “examinata,” appear in the registers only at the end of the 19th century, and at first only among newcomers: in 1888 in Jurków, for the child of an overseer of public roads; in 1897 in Dobra, in a family from elsewhere; and in 1900, for the child of a railway official. The register gives this last midwife’s name: Maria Hoffmann. Peasant children went on being delivered by neighbor women for a long time.

How certain is this

  • CertainThe new column from 1832, the notes “non examinata,” and the emergency baptism of 1829. Readings compared with the scans.
  • To be checkedThe numbers of births were counted automatically from the transcriptions. The same midwife is sometimes recorded in different ways, and two women may have had the same first name and surname.

What we don’t know

  • Who taught the midwives of our villages, and whether any of them later passed the examination.
  • How many births ended in the mother’s death. The death registers note this only sometimes, as “propter partum.”
