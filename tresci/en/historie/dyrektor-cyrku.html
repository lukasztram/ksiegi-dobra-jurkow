In the death register of the neighboring parish of Mszana Dolna, the same word stands next to almost every name: “agricola,” farmer. Under number 46 for 1907, the priest wrote something in parentheses that appears on no other of the several thousand pages of these registers.

That is all the register tells us about him. He died on Tuesday, August 20, and was buried on Thursday, August 22. The house-number column is empty, because the visitor did not live in any house in Mszana. He was a Catholic, and he was buried by the parish priest himself, Father Wincenty Jankowski.

What the entry itself says

The priest wrote the words “dyrektor cyrku,” circus director, in Polish, in the middle of a Latin sentence. The Latin of the parish registers had its own words for a farmer, a shoemaker, an innkeeper, and a beggar. It had none for a circus director.

He gave the cause of death in Polish too: “skrwawienie się z powodu rany,” bleeding to death from a wound. He did not write where the wound came from. In the same years, when someone had been killed, he wrote “zabity” or “occisus,” killed, and when someone died at work, he named what had killed them: a tree, a cart, a saw in a sawmill (the story {{tytul:las-trak-woda}}). Here there is no such word. The wound could have come from working with animals, from putting up a tent, or from a fight. The register does not settle it.

The priest wrote the place of birth as he heard it, breaking the Hungarian name into three words. Szentistvánfalva means “St. Stephen’s village.” Nitra County lay in the northern part of the Hungary of that time, in what is now western Slovakia. The village of that name is today’s Popudiny near Skalica, not far from the border of Moravia. It received its official Hungarian name in 1888, from the patron saint of its church. When Gustaw Farkas was born, it was still called Popudin. In 1910 it had 392 inhabitants, the vast majority of them Slovaks. In Hungarian, the surname Farkas means “wolf.”

There is a coincidence in this entry that the priest probably did not notice. In Hungary, August 20 is St. Stephen’s Day, the feast of the country’s first king and patron saint. A man born in St. Stephen’s village died on St. Stephen’s Day.

How a circus came to Mszana

From December 1884 the railroad from Chabówka to Nowy Sącz ran through Mszana Dolna (the story {{tytul:kolej}}). Mszana was a small town with a court, a post office, fairs, and a station, and Hungary lay just one mountain range away. Laborers went from Mszana to Hungary (the story {{tytul:peszt-chicago}}). In the other direction came merchants, tinkers, and, as we see, a circus. Large circuses traveled by rail in those days and could fill several dozen cars. How Farkas’s circus traveled is not known.

How certain is this

  • CertainThe content of the entry: first name and surname, occupation, age, dates of death and burial. I checked it against the scan.
  • ProbableThe reading of the cause of death: the first word is “skrwawienie,” possibly “okrwawienie.” That “Szen istvan falva” is Szentistvánfalva, that is, Popudiny: I found no other village of that name in Nitra County.
  • PossibleThat the circus came by rail and set up in Mszana at the time of the summer fairs.
  • To be checkedNewspapers from August 1907. In the “Kuryer Lwowski” (Lviv Courier) and in the Viennese papers that can be searched online, I found no mention of this death. It has not been possible to check the Kraków and Hungarian papers.

What we don’t know

  • How he was wounded.
  • What his circus was called and what became of it after its director’s death.
  • Whether he had a family. The register names neither a wife nor parents.
  • Where his grave is.
