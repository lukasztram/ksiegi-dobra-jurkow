In an ordinary year about 20 people died in Jurków and 16 in Chyszówki. In 1847, 111 people were buried in Jurków and 118 in Chyszówki. The registers of these villages never record so many deaths in a single year, before or after. In Gruszowiec, whose registers were transcribed in November 2026, 60 people died in 1847, almost five times as many as in an ordinary year.

The transcriptions of the other villages’ registers from November 2026 show that the famine struck the whole parish. In Porąbka 79 people died in 1847, seven times the usual number. In Łostówka 23 people were buried, more than five times the usual number. Wilczyce was hit hardest: 77 dead, when in an ordinary year eight or nine people died there. For 14 of them the priest wrote “ob famem,” of hunger. Only in Zadziele, the smallest village of the parish, was 1847 not the worst year: 13 people died there, and in 1873, 14 (as of November 2026).

The cause was the crop failure of 1846, after which famine spread across all of Galicia (the Austrian part of partitioned Poland). Death came in the summer, in the lean season before the harvest, when the stores had run out and the new crops were still a long way off. In Jurków 16 people died in June 1847 and 25 in July. In Chyszówki 82 people died between June and August.

The famine came right after the Galician Slaughter. In February 1846 peasants in western Galicia attacked manor houses, plundered them, and killed landowners and manorial officials. On March 1 of that year the death register of Dobra recorded the death of the mandatary, that is, the manor’s official, “as the result of a crime.” This is told in the story {{tytul:mandatariusz}}.

The priest most often entered “febris nervosa,” that is, typhus. He also entered dysentery and dropsy, which go hand in hand with hunger. Sometimes he wrote plainly: “ob famem,” of hunger.

House no. 29 in Wilczyce

House no. 29 in Wilczyce was home to landless lodgers, people without land of their own who lived by hiring out their labor. From June to the end of July 1847 eight people died there. On June 7 ten-year-old Jan Sędziak died of hunger, and on June 20 his 35-year-old mother Katarzyna. On July 11 Tomasz Pulka and his wife Zofia died of hunger on the same day. The priest recorded her as Tomasz’s widow, though she outlived him by only a few hours. They were buried together on July 13.

After the parents, their sons died: three-year-old Jakub on July 14 of dysentery and nine-year-old Jan on July 29 of hunger. Between them, on July 15, 37-year-old Kazimierz Pulka, also a landless lodger from this house, died of dysentery.

This time it was above all adults in the prime of life who died. In Jurków, people aged 15 to 49 made up 44 percent of that year’s dead, compared with about 19 percent in other years. In Chyszówki death came to 60 houses. In the Rataj family’s house, no. 92, six people died within twelve days in July.

Hungry people from other parts passed through the villages, and some of them died here. An unknown ten-year-old beggar girl died in Jurków in the house of Szymon Jania, a fifteen-year-old beggar at the widow Marianna Maludy’s, and ten-year-old Józef at Jan Podgórny’s. The priest did not know their surnames. In December a beggar woman from Słopnice died in the house of Wojciech Rapacz in Chyszówki, recorded only as “Catharina N.” The householders took strangers in under their roofs, though they were starving too. In Gruszowiec in June an unknown twenty-year-old beggar died of hunger in the house of Jan Miśkowiec, as told in the story {{tytul:gruszowiec}}.

The famine did not end with one year. In 1848 typhus took many more people, in 1849 cholera came, and in 1853–1855 the priest again wrote “ob miseriam,” of destitution. Those years are also the subject of the stories {{tytul:wielkie-sniegi}} and {{tytul:doktor-z-wiednia}}.

How certain is this

  • CertainThe scale of the disaster. It is shown by hundreds of records in the independent registers of eight villages of the parish.
  • CertainThe entries on the deaths of Tomasz and Zofia Pulka, checked against the scan.
  • To be checkedThe figures come from machine transcription, as of November 2026. I calculated the average for an ordinary year from 1830–1870, leaving out the years of famine, destitution, and cholera. Individual dates and names may be wrong, but this does not affect the overall picture.

What we don’t know

  • Where the children who came begging to Jurków and died here came from.
  • Whether the manor in Dobra helped the starving. A year earlier it had lost its mandatary, but by 1847 it had a new one. On June 30, 1847, a beggar woman from Kasina, a stranger, died of hunger at the manor, as told in the story {{tytul:cystersi}}. Whether the manor had taken her in, the register does not say.
