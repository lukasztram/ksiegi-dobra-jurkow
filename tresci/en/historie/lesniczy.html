Józef Przybytek is my direct ancestor, six generations back. From his daughter Kunegunda, born in 1840, a straight line leads to my grandmother Franciszka. I am a forester myself, so I read the record of his wedding in 1838 with particular interest.

On November 20, 1838, in the church in Dobra, thirty-year-old Józef, son of Andrzej and Agnieszka Przybytek, married Marianna, daughter of the late Józef Podgórny of Jurków. According to the record, the bride was 16. Next to the groom the priest wrote an occupation that hardly ever appears in the parish registers.

Marianna was an orphan, so consent to the marriage was given “a tutoribus et Dominis,” by her guardians and the manor. The witnesses were the leaseholder of the estate, Józef Kisielewski, and Mateusz Rudnicki, teacher and organist. The marriage was blessed by the parish priest, Ferdynand Wilczek. Company from the manor at a peasant wedding is no surprise: the groom was a man of the manor, and the bride’s family had long had close ties with the manor. I wrote about this in the story {{tytul:hrabia-przy-chrzcielnicy}}.

Józef was not a newcomer. The Przybytek family had been known in Dobra for more than a hundred years: around 1720 Wojciech Przybytek was the village head, and in 1739 Sebastian Przybytek was listed among the full-holding peasants. In 1850 Józef was the guardian of his wife’s younger brother, and under the consent to that brother’s marriage he made a cross, because he could not write. So he was more likely a local forest keeper from the village community than a trained professional who kept records. The term “magister silvarum” is rare. In other registers from Galicia (the Austrian part of partitioned Poland), foresters were called “praefectus silvarum,” “custos silvae,” or “sylvario.”

Seven and a half kreuzer per log

In 1711 the Dobra estate included about 7,000 morgs of forest (a morg was about 1.4 acres). On the spot, lumber was sawn, shingles and barrel staves were split, and tar was produced. When, around 1820, the peasants’ labor dues to the manor were converted into rent, the peasants could take firewood and building timber from the manor forests. They could also fell trees and haul the logs to the manor sawmill. The boards were theirs if they paid the lord of the manor 15 kreuzer (a small Austrian coin), “and the forester 7½ kreuzer, Vienna currency, for each log felled.” So recorded the Słownik geograficzny Królestwa Polskiego (Geographical Dictionary of the Kingdom of Poland), adding that this state of affairs lasted until 1848. The forester of Dobra thus earned money on every log his neighbors felled.

The forest law of those years required felling in December, January, and February, and the timber had to be hauled out by mid-April. When the forester caught a wood thief, he did not punish him himself but reported him to the manor. Instructions for forest staff wanted the forester to be “seldom at home, more often in the forest,” and forbade him to meet peasants in taverns.

After the peasants were given ownership of their land, the forester’s world changed. The Austrian Forest Act of 1852 and the imperial patent of 1853 required the peasants’ rights to the forest to be settled. In Galicia there were more than 32,000 disputes over forests and pastures, and the peasants lost about 30,000 of them. Around 1880 the manor in Dobra had 696 morgs of forest, and the peasants 414.

A day in the life of the forester, January 1840

This story combines facts with imagination. What concerns Dobra has a source above; the rest I filled in myself.

Józef leaves the house while it is still dark. He has been Marianna’s husband for fourteen months, and in a few weeks their daughter Kunegunda will be born. Winter is the working season in the forest. The farmers of Dobra are felling trees for their own boards, so the first task of the day is the count: Józef walks the felling sites, counts the fresh stumps, and checks whose logs lie by the road. For each one he is owed seven and a half kreuzer. He counts in his head or on a tally stick; the writing he leaves to the manor clerk.

In the evening he returns to the village, where everyone knows him. To his neighbors he is someone from the manor, but also one of their own: he has the same origins and the same neighbors. In his house a daughter will soon be born who, sixteen years later, will marry Antoni Podgórni and move to house no. 2 in Jurków. With her the line to my grandmother will begin.

How certain is this

  • CertainThe wedding date, the groom’s occupation, the consent of the guardians and the manor, the witnesses. The transcription was checked against the scan.
  • CertainThe forester’s fee per log. It is given in the Słownik geograficzny Królestwa Polskiego.
  • ProbableJózef could not write. He made a cross under the consent of 1850.
  • PossibleHe was a local forest keeper from the village community, not a trained professional.

What we don’t know

  • How long Józef served as forester and what he did after the labor dues to the manor were abolished.
  • Where his house stood. The cadastral map of Dobra from the mid-19th century is held in the National Archives in Kraków.
