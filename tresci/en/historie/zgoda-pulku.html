A soldier needed the written consent of his superiors to marry, and the priest had to see it before he blessed the marriage. The parish marriage registers contain more than a hundred and twenty entries with such notes, from the 1840s to 1925 (as of November 2026). More than thirty of them come from the registers of Porąbka, Wilczyce, Zadziele, and Łostówka, transcribed in November 2026. In them the priest noted who had issued the consent, its date, and the number of the document, and he filed the document itself in the parish records, in a fascicle, that is, a folder, under a running number.

The army also found its way into the registers at baptisms. In November 1823 in Gruszowiec, the godfather was Paweł Żeleski, recorded as “Dragonus Circuli Sandecensis,” dragoon of the Sącz circle. The circle (cyrkuł, Kreis) was a district of the Austrian administration, with its office in Nowy Sącz.

The army’s consent and the manor’s consent

On May 23, 1847, in the famine year, 25-year-old Paweł Kuchta of Półrzeczki, a soldier of the 7th Company of the Imperial-Royal Infantry Regiment No. 20, married 30-year-old Franciszka Smoleń of Chyszówki. Both of them needed permission.

The dominium, that is, the manor, at that time carried out the orders of the authorities in Galicia (the Austrian part of partitioned Poland) and took recruits for the army, as told in the story {{tytul:mandatariusz}}. Why the bride needed the manor’s consent, the priest did not write. Regiment No. 20, in which most men from the parish served, was then called the Hohenegg Regiment, after its honorary colonel.

Consents from Hungary

In the 1850s Regiment No. 20 already bore the name of the Prussian prince Friedrich Wilhelm. On September 23, 1857, the 30-year-old soldier Sebastian, son of Michał and Zofia, née Mycoń, married in Dobra. His consent came from far away.

Stuhlweissenburg is today’s Székesfehérvár in Hungary. The bride, 19-year-old Marianna, also needed consent, because she was a minor. It was issued by the Imperial-Royal district office in Tymbark, acting as the guardianship authority.

The consent came from wherever the regiment happened to be stationed. The notes in the marriage register of Dobra lead across the whole Kingdom of Hungary of that time:

  • 1857Stuhlweissenburg, today Székesfehérvár in Hungary.
  • 1859Košice, “in Cassovia,” today in Slovakia.
  • 1862Košice again, this time for a soldier of Infantry Regiment No. 67.
  • 1865Levoča, “Leutschau,” today in Slovakia.
  • 1866Pest, “Pesthini,” today part of Budapest.

Some of the consents were issued by the General Command in Lviv, “Generalis Commando Leopoli.”

After the defeat of 1866

In the summer of 1866 Austria lost the war with Prussia. Two years later, by a law of December 5, 1868, it introduced universal military service. Men served three years in the line, seven years in the reserve, and two years in the home defense force, the Landwehr.

In those years, at the weddings of young men, the priest recorded the consent of the Imperial-Royal district office (starostwo) in Limanowa “pro militari statu,” in military matters. From 1869 he cited the ordinance of December 5, 1868, in doing so. So it was at the wedding of 23-year-old Józef Lach and 20-year-old Helena Czech.

Both were minors, since the age of majority then began at 24. So their fathers, Antoni Lach and Józef Czech, also gave their consent. Under the Polish text of the consent they made crosses, “as being unable to write.”

A number in the military register

In the early 20th century the priest entered next to the groom his unit and the number of his sheet in the military record book, the “Grundbuchblatt.” Most often it was Infantry Regiment No. 20, but there were also soldiers of the pioneer battalion, artillerymen, supply-train soldiers, uhlans, and men of the home defense force. In May 1904, 24-year-old Józef Miśkowiec, “Ersatz Reservist Uhlanen,” a supplementary reservist of the uhlans, married in Gruszowiec. The father of the underage bride, Marianna Struga, signed his consent to the marriage with a cross.

A few years later the war broke out. The fallen are the subject of the story {{tytul:zolnierze}}.

Now in Poland

After 1918 the government and the language changed, but consent was still needed. In February 1919 the priest in Półrzeczki wrote in Polish: “Zezwolenie ze Starostwa co do wojskowości z dnia 27/I 1919,” permission from the district office regarding military matters, dated January 27, 1919. In 1921 permission was issued by the General District Command in Kraków, in 1922 by the District Recruiting Command in Nowy Sącz, and in 1925 by the command in Nowy Targ.

How certain is this

  • CertainThe entries cited from 1823, 1847, 1849, 1857, 1859, 1862, 1865, 1866, 1869, 1870, 1904, 1919, 1921, 1922, and 1925, checked against the scans. The date of the military service law and the length of service, according to Wikipedia.
  • ProbableThe number of entries with notes about the army, counted by machine and reviewed, as of November 2026. That the district office’s consents after 1868 concerned men liable for military service. The registers give only the paragraph of the ordinance, not its content.
  • To be checkedThe surnames of the soldier Sebastian of 1857 and his bride Marianna, and the surname of the dragoon of 1823.

What we don’t know

  • Why in 1847 the bride needed the manor’s consent.
  • How many couples did not marry because they did not get consent.
  • What the dragoon of the Sącz circle was doing in Gruszowiec.
