Death often broke up marriages halfway through. Whoever was left alone with children and a farm usually remarried quickly. In 1786–1899 a widower or a widow was one of the partners in more than one wedding in three in the parish.

A week after the funeral

Tomasz Chudziak of Półrzeczki, of house no. 58, was married to Maria, née Bielska. Three of their daughters died as small children: Regina in 1830, at just over a year old, and the twins Katarzyna and Zofia, born in April 1831, who died less than a month apart at the turn of 1832 and 1833. On January 29, 1837, Maria herself died of consumption. She was 35. Two-and-a-half-year-old Małgorzata was left at home.

A week after his wife’s death, on February 5, 1837, Tomasz married 22-year-old Jadwiga Mrozek of house no. 5.

Why so fast? Three days later, on February 8, was Ash Wednesday. No weddings were held during Lent, so the next chance would have come only after Easter. And at home there was a small child and a farm. When people married is the subject of the story {{tytul:wesela}}, and the consent of the dominium, that is, the manor’s administrative office, of the story {{tytul:mandatariusz}}.

Little Małgorzata died in June 1837, at the age of three. In August 1838 a daughter, Maria, was born to Tomasz and Jadwiga.

Widowers did not dawdle

Tomasz was not an exception. In the registers it has been possible to link more than three hundred deaths of wives with the remarriages of their husbands (as of November 2026). Half of the widowers remarried within about seven months of the wife’s death, and one in five within three months. Widowers also remarried more often than widows did: in 1786–1899 one groom in four was a widower, and one bride in five a widow.

A widow had to wait

With widows it was different. Of the more than 150 whose remarriage could be found, almost none married again within three months, and only one in seven within six months. Half waited more than thirteen months.

That was what the law required. The Austrian Civil Code, in force in Galicia (the Austrian part of partitioned Poland) from 1812, did not allow a pregnant widow to remarry before giving birth. If there was doubt about a pregnancy, she had to wait six months. The point was to avoid any dispute over whose child it was. If a pregnancy was unlikely, the district (Kreis) office could allow the marriage earlier, but not before three months had passed.

There were exceptions. On March 13, 1847, in the famine year, the 30-year-old landless lodger Maciej Trzópek died suddenly in Porąbka. On May 24, after 72 days, his 29-year-old widow Anastazja married a 33-year-old bachelor, Józef Trzópek. The priest recorded the consent of the manor in Janowice dated May 15. Whether there was also permission from the authorities, the register does not say.

An older groom, an older bride

A widower usually stood at the altar at about 42 and most often married a woman who had never been married, on average 14–15 years younger. A widow usually married at about 34–35. When she married a bachelor, he was most often three or four years younger than she was. For a bachelor without land, marrying a widow could mean a farm of his own.

In 1786–1849 a widower or widow took part in two weddings out of five, and in the second half of the 19th century in slightly more than one in three. How often small children died is the subject of the story {{tytul:pierwszy-rok}}.

What we don’t know

  • How many widowers and widows never remarried.
  • Whether the widows who remarried within six months had official permission to do so.
