This story goes beyond the parish of Dobra, to its neighbors. To the west lay the parish of Mszana Dolna. In its registers, Mszana Dolna, Mszana Górna, Kasinka Mała, Łętowe, Słomka, Glisne, and the part of Łostówka that did not belong to Dobra each have their own sections. For 1890–1916 the annual copies of these registers survive, that is, the copies the parish priest made after each year. In November 2026 they were transcribed in full: almost 15,000 baptisms, marriages, and burials.

They were transcribed in the course of researching my father’s family. These registers contain the Węglarz family of Mszana Górna, my great-grandmother’s family. My father’s family also handed down two stories: that our ancestors had once come from Hungary, and that there were aunts across the ocean. In the Mszana registers, Hungary and America come up every few pages. Nearly seventy entries lead far from home: about fifty to Hungary, twelve to America, a few to Ostrava, Silesia, and Prussia.

Died in Budapest

Between 1892 and 1911, nineteen men from the parish who died in Hungary were entered in the death registers, sixteen of them in Budapest. The parish priest did not bury them. He added them when a death certificate came from Hungary and the district office in Limanowa ordered it recorded. Such entries usually have neither a number nor the priest’s signature, and the remarks column gives the number of the official letter.

The first was Jan Łabuz, a miller from Mszana Dolna. He was 33 and married. He drowned in Budapest on September 30, 1892. Józef Bolisęga of Kasinka Mała, a married laborer, fell from a height in May 1894. He was 28. The letter from Budapest is dated January 27, 1895, eight months after his death.

Franciszek Krawczyk of Łostówka, a hired laborer, was killed in Budapest in May 1895. He was 39. Andrzej Kraska of Kasinka Mała drowned in March 1899; the priest wrote the cause in Polish: “utonął,” drowned. Walenty Szczypka of Mszana Dolna died of consumption in August 1898 in St. Stephen’s Hospital and was buried there. Antoni Kołodziejski of Kasinka Mała died in the same hospital in 1907, and Józef Mucha, also from Kasinka, in St. Roch’s Hospital in 1906.

The youngest of the nineteen was 17; most were between their early twenties and forty. Ten came from Kasinka Mała.

Among them is Maciej Węglarz of Mszana Górna, son of Józef and Katarzyna. He died on December 22, 1894, aged 27. The death certificate was issued in Budapest in February 1895. He bore my great-grandmother’s surname. Whether he was related to her, I do not know.

Kőbánya, or the quarry

What did they do there? The priests wrote briefly: laborer, hired hand, once a miller. More is known from newspapers and from the work of historians. In the late 1870s, fast-growing Budapest began to draw the poor from Galicia (the Austrian part of partitioned Poland). Poles worked in brickyards, mills, tanneries, and cement works, on building sites, and on the regulation of the Danube. “One emigrant draws a dozen or more of his own people after him,” wrote the “Kurjer Lwowski” (Lviv Courier) in 1898. Most Poles settled in the city’s tenth district, Kőbánya. Its name simply means “quarry.”

From 1897, Masses were said in Kőbánya for the Polish workers. In 1908 a permanent chaplain came to them, Father Wincenty Danek of the Archdiocese of Lviv. Two years later he asked his compatriots in America for help. In the “Dziennik Chicagoski” (Chicago Daily) he wrote that about 15,000 Poles were living in Budapest, “mostly just laborers who live by the work of their hands.”

The Mszana entries fit this picture: a miller, a laborer who fell from a height, two men who drowned. Besides, Budapest was closer to Mszana than it might seem. From December 1884 the railroad ran through here (the story {{tytul:kolej}}), and in Nowy Sącz it joined the line that led through Muszyna to Hungary.

Cholera in Kőrösmező

Jakub Krawczyk of Łostówka died far from Budapest. He was 28. He died of cholera on August 8, 1893, in Kőrösmező. This is today’s Yasinia in Ukraine’s Transcarpathia, then a Hungarian village right on the border of Galicia.

In 1893–1894 a railroad was being built there, deep into the Carpathians. Workers gathered from many places were housed in shared barracks, and in 1893 cholera broke out among them. In all, 230 of the line’s builders died. The surrounding villages would not let them be buried in their cemeteries, so the railroad bought land for separate ones. Those going home were examined by a doctor and carried in a separate car. Cars bound for Budapest were uncoupled at Kőbánya.

The register does not say that Jakub worked on this construction. But the place, the year, and the disease all fit. News of his death reached Mszana only in April 1895, twenty months later.

Born in Budapest

It was not only men who went to Hungary. The death registers of 1894–1912 include twenty-one children and young people next to whom the priest added: born in Budapest. Their parents came back to the village with them and buried them here.

Angela Niedośpiał died in Mszana Dolna in April 1901. She was three weeks old and had been born in Budapest, so her mother had set out on the journey with a newborn. Jan Kęskowicz, a nine-year-old born in Budapest, drowned the same month in Mszana Górna.

Jan Karpierz of Kasinka Mała died on September 29, 1902, aged six. The priest wrote down exactly where the boy came from.

The boy was taken by “sipek,” that is, diphtheria (the story {{tytul:sipka}}). Eleven days later his brother Józef, two and a half years old, died of the same disease.

Budapest also shows up in the marriage registers. Five brides from 1902–1913 were born in Budapest. Katarzyna Knapczyk of Łostówka was 21 in February 1902, so she was born around 1881. Her parents were thus already in Budapest in the early 1880s, right after the wave from Galicia had begun. Anna Mucha of Kasinka Mała was born in Budapest in March 1891. She married in February 1907, before she was even sixteen; the court in Mszana Dolna gave its consent.

Of the twenty-one born in Budapest, the priest recorded six as illegitimate. That is nearly one in three. Among all the children baptized in the parish, roughly one in sixteen was illegitimate. Father Danek reported from Budapest that many of his countrymen lived there without being married, and he himself married such couples.

In Hungary, moreover, people married differently than in Galicia. From October 1, 1895, only a marriage contracted before a civil official was valid there. In the summer of 1896 two couples from the parish married this way in Budapest. On July 27, Cecylia Łacna of Kasinka Mała married Andrzej Białobok, born in Siary. On August 23, Stefan Dziedzina of Łostówka married Franciszka Kotarba. The parish priest copied both marriages into the register in Mszana. There are no witnesses and no priest next to them; instead there is a sentence in German.

“Zabřech (Hungaria)”

The word “Hungaria” comes up in the Mszana registers so often that once the priest wrote it out of sheer habit. In August 1902 Stanisław Pach, a laborer from Vítkovice born in Dębno, married in Mszana Dolna. His bride was twenty-year-old Wiktoria Strzelec from house no. 234. Vítkovice is a great ironworks near Ostrava, in Moravia.

The Pachs’ first son, Władysław, died in December 1903 “in Zabrechu u Svinova” (in Zábřeh near Svinov), at six months old. The second, Bolesław, was born in October 1906 in Mszana, in his mother’s home, and died three and a half months later.

But Zábřeh is not in Hungary. It is a village right next to the Vítkovice ironworks, today a district of Ostrava.

In Mszana only a few entries lead to Ostrava and Silesia. Sebastian Grzesiak of Kasinka Mała, a miner, died of typhus in March 1893 in Orlová near Karviná, aged 26. In the parish of Dobra it was the other way around: there most of the notes led precisely to Ostrava (the story {{tytul:ostrawa}}).

To Chicago

The first trace of America appears in the same entry as Budapest. In July 1894, two-and-a-half-year-old Marianna Cieślak died in Mszana Dolna.

The girl was born in Budapest, her father went on, across the ocean, and her mother came back to Mszana with the child.

After that, America is absent from the registers for more than a decade. It returns in 1907 and from then on appears more and more often.

Chicago comes up most often, and within it the Polish parish of St. Casimir. Next to a boy from Słomka, baptized in 1907 as illegitimate, someone later added, in different ink, the father and the words “copul. Chicago par. S. Casimiri 10.X.1909.” So the parents married in Chicago and the child became legitimate. Józef Gajda of Łętowe and his wife Franciszka were also married at St. Casimir’s. They came back, and in December 1911 they had their daughter Wiktoria baptized in Mszana.

Two children were entered in the register in Mszana even though they had been born and baptized in Chicago. One of them in Polish.

On the adjacent page is Franciszek, son of Józef Potaczek of Mszana Górna, born on September 14, 1911, “natus et Baptisatus in Chicago in America,” born and baptized in Chicago in America. A boy from Łostówka, born in August 1907, was baptized in the Chicago Church of the Immaculate Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary; a slip with the certificate was later tucked between the pages of the register. Anna, daughter of Maciej Pustelnik of Mszana Dolna, was baptized in February 1910 in yet another church in Chicago.

St. Casimir’s parish was founded in 1890. Around 1910 it had more than two thousand families. Immaculate Conception parish, founded in 1882, was the first Polish parish in South Chicago, the steel mill district.

From America, as from Hungary, people came back with small children. Franciszek Dziedzina, son of Józef and Franciszka, née Jamróz, was born in Canada. He died of pneumonia in Mszana Dolna in June 1909, aged ten months. Angela Polak of Mszana Górna, “born in America and baptized there,” died in April 1912. She was six months old.

Not everyone came back. In November 1910 a 44-year-old farmer from Mszana Dolna died in America. Regina Kapałka remarried in February 1913. For her to do so, the bishop’s consistory in Tarnów had to rule that the death of her husband Franciszek “has been sufficiently established by a death certificate issued in America.” In October 1916, in the middle of the war, Wiktoria Potaczek of Mszana Górna married. The priest noted: “The death certificate of Klemens Potaczek, who died in Uniontown, confirmed by the branch office of the Imperial-Royal Consulate in Pittsburg, is in the files.” Uniontown is a town in the coal and coke region of Pennsylvania.

How people traveled

The parish registers say nothing about the journey itself. American papers do. I have two such documents for a girl from the neighboring parish of Niedźwiedź. Zofia Halama of Konina was 18 when she came ashore in Philadelphia on September 17, 1913. Konina is my grandmother’s village, and the Halamas are her family. Whether Zofia was related to her, I do not know.

The immigration officer wrote down what no parish record contains. Zofia was a servant and single, and she could read and write. She was five feet one inch tall, that is, 155 centimeters, with a fair complexion, blond hair, and blue eyes. She had paid for her ticket herself and had 25 dollars with her. Her father, Jakub, had stayed behind in Konina. She was going to her sister Karolina, who already lived in Philadelphia at 1100 North Front Street. Right below her on the passenger list is Anna Hanula, also eighteen and also from Konina.

The same page of the list records Czechs and Moravians, a German from Hungary, a Polish family with a three-year-old daughter, and a group of Jews from Russia. In the year ending June 30, 1913, immigration officials recorded more than 185,000 arrivals as Poles.

Zofia and Anna sailed from Bremen on the steamer “Wittekind.” She was built in 1894 in Hamburg for the Norddeutscher Lloyd line, from the start to carry emigrants and cargo. After a refit in 1900 she carried 177 second-class passengers and over a thousand in steerage, that is, the cheapest class. She made 12 knots, that is, 22 kilometers per hour, so the Atlantic crossing took nearly two weeks.

Less than a year after Zofia’s voyage, war broke out. The “Wittekind” took refuge in Boston and was interned there. In 1917 the Americans seized her. As the “Freedom” she carried soldiers home from France in 1919, and in 1924 she was scrapped.

On the passenger list, next to Zofia’s name, there is a stamp dated October 23, 1940. An official was then checking when and on what ship she had arrived. So Zofia stayed in America.

And then France

Next to seven children baptized in 1893–1907, civil registrars added many years later that they had died in France. That is the next wave of departures, after the First World War, and another story.

And the family story about Hungarian roots? It may have come from exactly this. You did not have to come from Hungary to have someone in the family who was born in Budapest, married there, or stayed there for good.

How certain is this

  • CertainAll the entries cited with first names and surnames, with their dates, places, and causes of death. I checked each one against the scan. Zofia Halama’s details come from her arrival card and the passenger list.
  • ProbableThe numbers of entries: counted from the transcriptions of all the annual copies, as of November 2026. That Jakub Krawczyk worked on the railroad construction in Kőrösmező. That Marianna Cieślak’s father went to America from Budapest. That the voyage on the “Wittekind” took nearly two weeks, and that the 1940 stamp is connected with Zofia’s application for citizenship.
  • PossibleThat the drownings and the fall from a height were accidents at work. That the family story about ancestors from Hungary comes from people going away for work. The comparison of illegitimate children rests on small numbers.
  • To be checkedThe name of the church in Chicago for the 1910 baptism. The place names in four entries about deaths in Hungary and America, which we know only from careless copies made in 1937.

What we don’t know

  • How many people really went away. The registers record only those who died, married, or had a child baptized abroad.
  • Why people from Mszana went mainly to Budapest, and those from neighboring Dobra to Ostrava. And why so many left Kasinka Mała.
  • Whether Jakub and Franciszek Krawczyk of Łostówka were brothers.
  • Whether Maciej Węglarz was related to my great-grandmother, and Zofia Halama to my grandmother. The registers of the parish of Niedźwiedź for those years are not online.
